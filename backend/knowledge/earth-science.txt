Earth is a differentiated, hyper-dense planet with an average density of approximately 5.515 grams per cubic centimeter, making it the densest planet in the solar system. This density arises from the planet's layered internal structure, formed early in its history through a process called planetary differentiation, where heavier elements sank toward the center while lighter materials rose toward the surface.

The core of the Earth is composed primarily of iron and nickel, and is divided into two distinct layers. The inner core is solid despite extreme temperatures of around 5,200 degrees Celsius, because immense pressure keeps the iron-nickel alloy from melting. The outer core, by contrast, is liquid, and its churning motion generates Earth's magnetic field through a process called the geodynamo effect.

Surrounding the core is the mantle, a thick layer of hot, slowly flowing rock that makes up roughly 84 percent of Earth's total volume. Convection currents within the mantle drive plate tectonics, the process responsible for continental drift, earthquakes, and volcanic activity.

The crust is Earth's thin outer shell, ranging from about 5 kilometers thick under the oceans to 70 kilometers thick under mountain ranges. It is broken into large tectonic plates that float on the mantle and shift slowly over geological time, colliding, separating, and sliding past one another.

Earth's atmosphere is composed of roughly 78 percent nitrogen, 21 percent oxygen, and trace amounts of argon, carbon dioxide, and other gases. This atmosphere, combined with Earth's distance from the Sun, allows liquid water to exist on the surface, a condition believed to be essential for life as we know it.

Earth's magnetic field, generated deep within the outer core, extends far into space and forms the magnetosphere. This field protects the planet from most of the Sun's charged particles, known as solar wind, which would otherwise strip away the atmosphere over time, as is believed to have happened to Mars.

The Moon, Earth's only natural satellite, plays a significant role in stabilizing Earth's axial tilt, which in turn stabilizes the planet's climate over long timescales. The Moon's gravitational pull is also the primary driver of ocean tides.
